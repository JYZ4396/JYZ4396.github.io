Beyond construction-debris counts

Volunteer cleanup records document what people collect after a hurricane. To understand which communities those observations represent, I studied both the number of construction items volunteers reported and where they submitted reports before and after each storm.

Comparing the same county before and after a storm

I linked Ocean Conservancy cleanup records to hurricane wind exposure and county housing data in Florida, Georgia, North Carolina, and South Carolina. For five hurricanes from 2022 to 2024, I compared reported items per cleanup in the 30 days before and after each storm. I used weighted Poisson models with a separate fixed effect for each county and hurricane combination.

Which communities entered the comparison?

I measured housing vulnerability using the shares of mobile homes and homes built before 1980. In the high-vulnerability group, 7.5% of county–hurricane pairs had eligible cleanup records on both sides of the storm. In the low-vulnerability group, 32.3% did. The pooled estimate of the change in reported debris counts was positive but imprecise. The coverage gap limits what that estimate can tell us about the most vulnerable counties.

Checking the result

I repeated the analysis with different time windows, wind thresholds, and minimum numbers of cleanup dates. Hurricane Idalia showed the clearest increase in reported items per cleanup, although its size depended on particular counties and cleanup dates.
